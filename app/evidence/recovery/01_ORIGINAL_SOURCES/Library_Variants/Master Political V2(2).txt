### **Special Front Page — 11 October 1993**
### **ALTIS BLEEDS INTO STALEMATE**  
### **Five Days of Battle Leave Turkish Lodgment Isolated, Greek Counterpressure Rising, Diplomats Moving In**

**By Elena Virelli, RBD War Desk**

**ALTIS, AEGEAN** — After five days of savage fighting, the battle for Altis has hardened into a brutal localized stalemate, with Turkish troops still holding the airport terminal and immediate perimeter but losing control of the island’s dominant radar ridge after repeated Greek assaults.

The original Turkish operation, launched after Ankara’s strikes on suspected military systems on the island, aimed to seize key points quickly and force a political settlement before the crisis spread. Instead, the fighting has settled into a blood-soaked deadlock.

The radar site itself is now a ruin, destroyed first by Turkish missile strikes and then rendered unusable through Greek demolition. The runway has been cratered and remains out of service, denying either side the ability to convert the airfield into a fixed-wing lifeline.

### **Air Battle Ends in No Decision**
Neither side now controls the skies over Altis. Turkish and Greek aircraft have both suffered losses, and after yesterday’s renewed dogfighting, direct overflight of the island has sharply decreased. The result is not peace, but paralysis.

### **Turkish Foothold Under Severe Pressure**
Turkish lead elements, inserted by helicopter in the opening phase of the battle, paid heavily for the assault. Parts of the initial landing force were cut down almost immediately under concentrated defensive fire near the airfield.

Though Turkish troops eventually secured the terminal complex, they were unable to hold the radar ridge. Greek forces, reinforced in the first hours of the crisis, fought their way back onto the high ground and have used mortars and probing attacks to keep the Turkish perimeter under constant pressure.

### **Greek Recovery, But No Breakthrough**
Greek defenders have regained confidence but not total control. Their early-response forces have taken severe losses, and despite repeated attacks they have not broken the Turkish position around the terminal. Heavier Greek follow-on forces remain largely uncommitted, a sign that Athens may be seeking to avoid the kind of escalation that would drag the wider region into war.

### **No Mainland Strikes, No Naval Clash**
For now, the most important fact remains what has **not** happened.

There have been:
- no strikes on the Greek or Turkish mainland,
- no major naval engagement,
- no widening of the conflict beyond Altis itself.

That restraint, however reluctant, is the only reason the crisis remains politically containable.

### **Diplomacy Takes Over as Ammunition Runs Low**
Military sources indicate both sides are now suffering severe sustainment problems on the island. Ammunition is running low, medical capacities are strained, and neither side appears capable of achieving a clean battlefield decision without accepting much larger political risks.

At the United Nations, discussions are accelerating around an international commission and ceasefire mechanism, while outside powers push both governments toward a settlement neither will openly admit they now need.

Altis remains contested.  
But the battle is no longer about victory alone.  
It is now about who can stop without looking defeated.

# Post-Battle Diplomatic Fallout Sheet  
## Altis Crisis — Day 5 Assessment

## 1. United Nations
**Position:** Immediate ceasefire, fact-finding, deconfliction, access.

The UN view is simple:
- Turkey escalated too fast.
- Greece contributed to the crisis by allowing the island to become militarily relevant.
- Both sides now need to stop before the conflict escapes the island.

### Likely UN moves
- emergency commission,
- ceasefire observers,
- humanitarian and prisoner access demands,
- pressure for a demilitarized buffer around the airfield and ridge.

### UN judgment
Turkey gets blamed for the **speed** of escalation.  
Greece gets blamed for the **conditions** that made escalation possible.

That is the cynical middle ground.

---

## 2. United States
**Position:** Angry at Turkey, worried about Greece, focused on containment.

Washington does not want to reward Turkey for launching quickly, but it also does not want Greece to widen the war and turn a contained crisis into a NATO nightmare.

### Private US line to Turkey
- Stop now.
- No second landing.
- No mainland strikes.
- No naval expansion.
- Accept monitoring.

### Private US line to Greece
- Do not counter-escalate.
- Do not trigger a theater-wide war over an island fight you are currently politically winning.

### US assessment
Turkey has tactical initiative but is drifting toward strategic embarrassment.  
Greece has narrative advantage but must not get arrogant.

---

## 3. USSR
**Position:** Opportunistic condemnation.

The Soviets are loving this politically.

They get to say:
- NATO-space is unstable,
- Turkey is reckless,
- the West cannot control its own periphery.

But they do **not** want to jump in directly unless someone gives them an excuse.

### Soviet likely behavior
- harder rhetoric,
- more naval observation,
- more intelligence fishing,
- more diplomatic pressure against Turkey.

### What they really want
Not war.  
Leverage.

---

## 4. NATO / Western Europe
**Position:** Freeze the map, stop the shooting, avoid choosing a winner.

Nobody serious wants to own this crisis.
The alliance position becomes:
- stop further operations,
- prevent a second Turkish wave,
- keep Greece from launching retaliatory attacks elsewhere,
- get everyone into a procedural mess instead of a live war.

### French / Italian view
- alarmed by instability,
- focused on shipping and regional balance,
- not emotionally invested in either side’s battlefield pride.

---

## 5. Greece
**Political position:** Improving.

Athens can now say:
- Turkey attacked first,
- Greek forces held,
- the island was not overrun,
- Turkish troops are stuck and bleeding.

That is powerful.

### Greek internal debate
There will be two camps:
- **the hardliners:** finish them, commit G3, destroy the lodgment;
- **the cautious bloc:** hold, bleed them, let diplomacy force Turkey into a bad exit.

The cautious bloc is more realistic right now because a total Greek counteroffensive risks forcing Turkey into wider escalation.

---

## 6. Turkey
**Political position:** mixed, dangerous, unstable.

Ankara can still tell its public:
- we proved Altis mattered,
- we struck the island,
- we landed troops,
- we did not back down.

But inside the state, the conversation is getting ugly.

Because the military question is now:
> “Did we seize a bargaining chip, or did we strand a company for pride?”

That is the real crisis.

---

## 7. Reunion
**Best posture:** involved, but not exposed.

Reunion’s smart move is not combat.  
It is relevance.

### Most realistic Reunion options
- offer talks or technical monitoring,
- quietly share intelligence,
- posture publicly as pro-stability,
- privately lean toward whichever outcome best limits Soviet gain.

### What Reunion should not do
- direct intervention,
- overt escort or military backing,
- anything that lets the USSR paint Reunion as another reckless regional escalator.

# What the Turks are considering now

This is where your “more Arma” comes in, and this is also where realism matters.

Turkey is not deciding between “win” and “lose.”
Turkey is deciding which kind of **damage** it can live with.

## Option 1: Hold and negotiate
This is the smartest political move.

Turkey keeps:
- terminal perimeter,
- surviving troops,
- claim of partial success.

Then pushes:
- ceasefire,
- observers,
- negotiated security language,
- face-saving exit.

### Why they’d choose it
Because the lodgment is degrading and they know it.

### Why they hate it
Because it feels like stopping short.

---

## Option 2: Emergency resupply / CASEVAC attempts
This is extremely likely.

Not a giant second invasion.  
More like:
- small runs,
- risky relief attempts,
- wounded extraction,
- ammunition pushes,
- night movement.

### Why they’d choose it
Because commanders on the ground are running dry.

### Why it’s dangerous
Because one failed relief effort can turn a contained stalemate into a fresh disaster.

This is probably your best Arma continuation:
- tense,
- plausible,
- desperate,
- still localized.

---

## Option 3: Limited commando-style action
Turkey may consider:
- silencing Greek mortar positions,
- retaking or raiding the ridge,
- destroying Greek support assets,
- opening a temporary corridor.

### Why they’d choose it
Because they need breathing room without committing to a full second wave.

### Why it fits
This is probably the most playable “more Arma” path while staying believable.

---

## Option 4: Second insertion / major reinforcement
They are absolutely considering it.

But this is the threshold decision.

### Why they want it
Because a trapped foothold with no sustainment is not a strategy.

### Why they hesitate
Because this is the move that risks:
- naval escalation,
- broader diplomatic backlash,
- the end of containment.

So yes, they are considering it.  
No, it is not the most likely immediate choice unless the current position starts collapsing fast.

---

## Option 5: Leave behind the seeds of a proxy problem
Even if Turkey freezes the battle, it may already be shifting toward the next phase:
- cultivating pro-Turkish committees,
- identifying collaborators,
- smuggling small arms later,
- building a post-battle deniable network.

That is realistic.  
That is long game.  
That is not a Day 5 battlefield fix.
