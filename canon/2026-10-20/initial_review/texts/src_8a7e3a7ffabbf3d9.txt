[1] REUNION BROADCASTING DAILY (RBD)
 Morning Edition
 Dateline: Reunion City | 13 September 1992 | 06:00
[2] “THE CLOCK IS RUNNING OUT”
[3] Ceasefire Nears Expiry as Debt Shock Ripples Through Markets; Parliament Deadlocked
[4] Reunion wakes today with two crises converging into one national test: a ceasefire in Dalmatia set to expire tomorrow night, and a sudden confidence shock after the government signed a major U.S. stabilization loan under strict conditions.
[5] With the Dalmatian front still unsettled and the Assembly unable to produce coherent emergency legislation, the country’s mood has shifted from anxiety to something sharper: fear that the state is losing control of both the battlefield and the balance sheet at the same time.
[6] CEASEFIRE ENDS TOMORROW AT 23:06
[7] The four-day ceasefire signed at 18:04 earlier this week expires 14 September at 23:06, and no extension has been announced. Government spokesmen have repeated that Reunion “seeks calm but will defend its territory,” while Croatian forces remain poised along key approaches and militia activity inside Dalmatia continues to be reported.
[8] Defense officials, speaking under attribution last night, described the strategic situation as “stable but brittle,” warning that a single large incident could trigger renewed escalation with little warning.
[9] THE LOAN THAT SHOOK THE CAPITAL
[10] The President and Finance Minister have signed a U.S. sovereign stabilization facility valued at $90 million USD (approximately 522 million RMC at the government’s stated exchange convention of $1 = 5.8 RMC).
[11] The facility carries a 12% fixed interest rate, a 3% upfront fee, disbursement by tranches, and a dedicated debt service mechanism tied to state revenues. The agreement also includes embedded monitoring and audit access, strict prohibited-use clauses, and the possibility of a later rate reduction only after extended compliance.
[12] Supporters describe the facility as a lifeline to prevent fiscal collapse and to stabilize procurement for reconstruction and basic sustainment. Critics call it a “foreign leash” imposed at the worst possible moment, arguing that signing first and seeking parliamentary settlement later has shredded confidence across political camps.
[13] FEARMONGERING OR REALITY: “DEPRESSION TALK” SPREADS
[14] The most dangerous element now is not any single clause in the agreement, but the speed at which catastrophic narratives are spreading.
[15] Across the capital’s financial district yesterday afternoon, rumors of “a coming collapse” raced faster than official explanations. Several banks tightened commercial lending windows. Importers demanded shorter payment terms. Some insurers began quietly reclassifying Dalmatian-related shipments as “high risk.” Contractors with government exposure moved to protect themselves by refusing new work unless payments were guaranteed.
[16] In the streets, the consequences were immediate.
[17] RBD has confirmed a wave of abrupt closures and “temporary shutters” among smaller retailers—especially those dependent on imported inputs or narrow cash flow—driven by three pressures at once:
[18] Credit tightening: local banks reducing exposure and demanding stronger collateral.
[19] Inventory risk: suppliers requiring faster settlement or cash, fearing currency instability.
[20] Consumer panic spending: households hoarding essentials and delaying discretionary purchases, leaving many shops with sales volatility and unusable stock.
[21] RBD has also received multiple reports of severe distress among financial professionals in the capital, including reports of suicides circulating through banker and broker networks overnight. Authorities have not released official numbers, and RBD will not repeat unverified claims. Still, the atmosphere in the district has been described by witnesses as “grim, rattled, and exhausted,” with some office buildings reducing hours and security increasing at entrances.
[22] “MOM-AND-POP IS GETTING CRUSHED”
[23] In Torrental and Steinberg, shopkeepers described the same chain reaction: customers buying staples in bulk while cutting back on everything else; distributors raising prices or requiring immediate payment; and landlords unwilling to extend rent grace periods amid uncertainty.
[24] A bakery owner in Steinberg told RBD at closing time:
 “We can still bake. Flour comes in. The problem is we don’t know what we’ll pay for it next week, and the bank is acting like I’m a gambler for asking for the same credit line I’ve had for ten years.”
[25] A hardware supplier in Reunion City put it more bluntly:
 “The state is late, the banks are scared, the wholesalers want cash, and my customers are hoarding. That’s how you kill small business without firing a shot.”
[26] PARLIAMENT: EMERGENCY SESSIONS, NO RESULTS
[27] The Assembly has convened repeated “emergency sessions” since the Dalmatian setback and the signing of the loan, but as of this morning there is still no ratified framework governing:
[28] the loan’s domestic legal standing and safeguards,
[29] emergency appropriations for war sustainment and logistics resilience,
[30] or the ruleset needed to prevent detentions and security operations from igniting political blowback in Dalmatia.
[31] Party blocs remain locked in a three-way fight:
[32] RRP institutionalists want swift ratification with guardrails to restore credibility.
[33] LUP demands social protections and opposes any mechanism that could starve civilian spending.
[34] RCA–NSF wants sovereignty limits on monitor access and maximum internal security flexibility.
[35] Behind closed doors, MPs describe the situation as “panic bargaining,” where everyone knows time is short but nobody wants to be left holding responsibility.
[36] CURRO: “THE MINISTER WHO DELIVERS,” OR “THE MINISTER WHO OVERREACHES”?
[37] Finance Minister Emilio Curro remains a central figure in the public imagination—praised by some as the only official who can “keep the state running,” condemned by others as an unelected technocrat with too much reach.
[38] Curro’s dual role—overseeing national economic policy while embedded in Dalmatian logistics—has drawn both admiration and unease. Parliament is expected to demand formal boundaries around any irregular logistics efforts and to tighten procurement channels as the loan’s oversight provisions come into force.
[39] WHAT HAPPENS NEXT
[40] Everything now points to two deadlines:
[41] Today: whether Parliament can produce any functional framework for spending triage, procurement discipline, and public reassurance.
[42] Tomorrow night: whether the ceasefire expires into renewed escalation—or into an extended pause.
[43] Until then, the markets are not waiting. Neither are the public, the ports, or the front.
[44] RBD Editorial Note: Panic can become policy if it’s allowed to spread unchallenged. Reunion requires clear numbers, clear rules, and clear leadership in daylight—before rumor does the governing for us.
