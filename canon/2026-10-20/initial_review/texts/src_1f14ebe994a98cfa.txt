[1] 20 AUG – 5 SEP 1992: EVENTS LOG
[2] 20–22 AUGUST: THE SIGNINGS LAND, THE SYSTEM LURCHES
[3] 20 Aug (Thu)
[4] Donahue signs the full stack: DPERA, SRRSAA, VRMHSA, NISASA, PCOUFSA, DTGRA, TFPS-GRA, GETIA 1992.
[5] Ministries issue “Implementation Directives” within 24 hours. Every committee chair in Parliament immediately schedules oversight hearings.
[6] 21 Aug (Fri)
[7] Interior issues the first NISASA implementing circular: defining “hostile propaganda,” detention paperwork rules, and how cases route into National Security Chambers.
[8] Justice quietly publishes interpretive guidance to judges (very conservative wording, trying to avoid legal anarchy).
[9] 22 Aug (Sat)
[10] Dalmatian ports begin DPERA “Phase 0” work: emergency road patching, crane inspections, water/sewage priority repairs.
[11] Panic buying in Dalmatian coastal towns accelerates. Inland relatives receive a visible flow of short-term evacuees “just for a few weeks.”
[13] 23–27 AUGUST: NISASA’S FIRST TESTS, DALMATIA’S FIRST CONTRACTS
[14] 23 Aug (Sun)
[15] First public NISASA enforcement operation: several arrests framed as “subversion network coordination.” The government does not name the networks; opposition media fills the vacuum with speculation.
[16] 24 Aug (Mon)
[17] Parliament reconvenes for the first post-signing session. The chamber is loud and ugly:
[18] LUP demands immediate limits on detention conditions and court secrecy.
[19] NSF and RCA hammer “deterrence” and “war readiness.”
[20] RRP tries to reframe the week as “reconstruction first, security bounded.”
[21] 25 Aug (Tue)
[22] First DPERA tenders are published. Local Dalmatian contractors surge in registration.
[23] A minor scandal breaks: one contractor appears linked to a known Dalmatian smuggling family. It’s not proven illegal—just politically radioactive.
[24] 26 Aug (Wed)
[25] Small UDF-style sabotage: a roadside culvert on a supply route is blown at night. No casualties; convoy rerouted.
[26] Government announces stricter convoy protocol and “non-negotiable fire discipline” for troops.
[27] 27 Aug (Thu)
[28] Mood in the capital splits:
[29] “The state is moving again” vs “the state is turning into a machine that can’t be stopped.”
[31] 28–31 AUGUST: DTGRA TURNS INTO A KNIFE FIGHT
[32] 28 Aug (Fri)
[33] DTGRA implementation begins. The central government requests candidate lists for the 40% appointed seats.
[34] Dalmatian municipal leaders start lobbying openly for “local legitimacy” and “no parachuted appointees.”
[35] 29 Aug (Sat)
[36] Croatian artillery “exercises” intensify. More rounds land in no man’s land. Still no direct casualties, but the psychological impact is heavy.
[37] Dalmatian local radio warns of fuel shortages even when depots remain stocked—driving more hoarding.
[38] 30 Aug (Sun)
[39] A publicized “humanitarian convoy” from a Croatian-linked NGO attempts to cross into the zone. Reunion turns it back citing security screening requirements.
[40] Croatia denounces it as obstruction; Reunion frames it as “covert supply potential.” The propaganda battle becomes constant.
[41] 31 Aug (Mon)
[42] A Dalmatian port work slowdown occurs (unofficial, a few hours), driven by fear of escalation and rumors of incoming shelling.
[43] The government urges calm and promises security, but avoids promising timelines.
[45] 1–3 SEPTEMBER: THE “THREE FRONTS” WEEK
[46] This is where Reunion gets pulled into three simultaneous conflicts: security legitimacy, Dalmatian governance, and economic anxiety.
[47] 1 Sep (Tue)
[48] TFPS-GRA visibly dampens food price spikes in Reunion City and Torrental, but retailers complain of uncertainty.
[49] Farmers’ associations begin organizing against “export restrictions,” calling it market interference.
[50] 2 Sep (Wed)
[51] First high-profile legal challenge to a NISASA detention reaches the courts. It becomes a national media spectacle.
[52] Civil-liberties lawyers accuse Interior of “testing the boundaries of lawful detention.” Interior says the case involves “active hostile coordination.”
[53] 3 Sep (Thu)
[54] A major Dalmatian incident: an IED attack hits a road crew working on DPERA-linked repairs.
[55] Limited casualties (usually 1–3 dead, several wounded) and the story explodes.
[56] Immediate consequence: road works pause for 24–48 hours; security escorts become mandatory.
[57] This is the first moment where reconstruction visibly collides with insurgency tactics.
[59] 4–5 SEPTEMBER: THE NATIONAL MOOD HARDENS
[60] 4 Sep (Fri)
[61] Parliament pushes emergency committee hearings on:
[62] Contractor vetting
[63] RIA conduct rules
[64] Compensation for affected families
[65] Whether Dalmatia should be treated as “civil reconstruction” or “counterinsurgency theater”
[66] LUP demands stronger civilian safeguards; NSF demands broader authority and faster detention powers in-theater; RRP tries to keep the coalition from splitting; RCA publicly supports “discipline and legitimacy” simultaneously (which is easy to say and hard to execute).
[67] 5 Sep (Sat)
[68] In Dalmatia, you see the most telling shift: fewer public demonstrations, more quiet behavior changes.
[69] Shop shelves cycle faster.
[70] Bus stations are crowded.
[71] People speak less freely in cafés.
[72] Nationalists become more confident in passive intimidation.
[73] In Reunion City, the mood becomes:
[74] “We’re rebuilding”
[75] “We’re being watched”
[76] “Dalmatia could drag us back into war.”
[78] Summary of What Changed by 5 September
[79] Dalmatia
[80] Reconstruction started, but the first serious attack on a reconstruction-linked target forces “security-first reconstruction.”
[81] DTGRA implementation becomes the central political battleground: appointments and legitimacy matter more than the law text.
[82] Security / Civil Liberties
[83] NISASA is no longer theoretical. By early September it has:
[84] real detainees
[85] real court challenges
[86] real media polarization
[87] Every future scandal will hit Donahue directly, even if Interior is the implementer.
[88] Economy
[89] TFPS-GRA reduces price panic, but creates a new political fault line: producers vs consumers, and “temporary stabilization” vs “permanent controls.”
[90] Rail procurement becomes a symbol: modern future vs debt and foreign dependence.
[91] RBDN SPECIAL REPORT — DALMATIA DESK
[92] 20 August 1992
 CROATIAN BUILDUP, HELICOPTER SHOOTDOWN, AND REUNION’S FORCE SURGE SHAKE DALMATIA
 Panic buying spreads in coastal towns as government urges “calm but firm vigilance”
[93] DALMATIA / REUNION CITY — A sharp rise in Croatian troop activity along the Dalmatian frontier, paired with a deadly air-defense incident on 11 August, has pushed Reunion-held Dalmatia into its most tense posture since the 1991 intervention. The Ministry of Defense confirmed that additional Croatian brigades have moved into forward staging areas opposite Reunion territory, accompanied by stepped-up artillery drills and a marked increase in militia activity inside the disputed strip.
[94] The same week, Reunion authorities ordered a significant reinforcement into theater—8,500 additional troops, including heavier armored elements and jet support—after what officials describe as a deliberate engagement against a Reunion helicopter operating within Reunion-administered territory.
[95] The result, across Dalmatian towns, is a rapid shift in civilian behavior: panic buying, inward migration, and a brittle public mood.
[97] Border posture hardens after August 11 shootdown
[98] The Ministry of Defense stated that a Reunion Lynx helicopter was shot down on 11 August during what it called a routine patrol inside Reunion-administered territory, less than two kilometers from the front line. Croatia has described the incident as an accident during training; Reunion disputes this, citing flight tracking and radar data that “strongly indicate deliberate engagement.”
[99] Officials have not publicly released the crew’s condition.
[100] In the days following, defense authorities expanded air and ground coverage, citing the need to prevent a “correction operation” against Reunion supply lines and urban nodes.
[102] Artillery “exercises” spill into no man’s land
[103] Local garrisons and residents have reported Croatian artillery rounds landing in uninhabited areas of no man’s land inside Dalmatia. No casualties were reported, but the Foreign Ministry filed a formal protest describing the incidents as reckless. Reunion forces have been instructed to maintain strict fire discipline to avoid escalation.
[105] UDF activity escalates: propaganda, sabotage, and heavy weapons sightings
[106] Internal security sources confirm increased activity by the Unified Dalmatian Front (UDF), a Croatian-aligned nationalist militia umbrella.
[107] Publicly acknowledged developments now include:
[108] Distribution of propaganda leaflets urging resistance with slogans such as “Reunion will bleed dry” and “They will flee like the Soviets from Afghanistan.”
[109] Reconnaissance sightings of UDF elements accompanied by armored vehicles—including what were described as T-72 tanks—near remote crossings and back routes.
[110] Multiple attempted disruptions of supply convoys and isolated outposts over the last two weeks, largely without casualties.
[111] Security officials describe the activity as “limited and sporadic,” but say the appearance of heavier systems in militia hands represents “a new phase of the conflict.”
[113] Smuggling incident deepens diplomatic crisis
[114] The government publicly confirmed interception of a Croatian-registered cargo ship entering a Dalmatian port under the guise of construction delivery. A search uncovered anti-tank guns, recoilless rifles, heavy machine guns, and ammunition believed destined for UDF networks.
[115] Reunion filed a diplomatic complaint in Zagreb, calling the operation a deliberate destabilization attempt. Croatia has not issued a formal public response.
[117] Civil sentiment: fear, fatigue, and fragile confidence
[118] RBDN reporting from coastal districts indicates civilian confidence remains brittle after the force surge.
[119] Current trend (as of this week)
[120] General mood: war fatigue and fear of renewed fighting; people assume “something bigger” may be coming.
[121] Panic behavior: widespread hoarding of bread staples, fuel canisters, and bottled water; families moving children inland toward relatives.
[122] Support base: port workers, merchants, and pragmatic locals show cautious relief due to increased naval and air presence—“better Reunion than chaos.”
[123] Neutral/autonomist bloc: growing anxiety; reduced cooperation with authorities due to fear of retaliation and uncertainty about long-term control.
[124] Pro-Croat nationalists: outward calm but rising passive hostility; increased likelihood of low-level sabotage and attempts to provoke security incidents.
[125] Key risk flagged by multiple local leaders: heavy-handed detentions or abusive policing could unify fence-sitters against Reunion and hand UDF a propaganda victory.
[127] Public order risk factors: next 30–60 days
[128] Security sources and municipal leaders describe several likely triggers:
[129] Likely triggers
[130] Curfews and checkpoints that disrupt commerce
[131] High-profile detainee abuse allegations or “disappearances”
[132] Fuel or bread shortages caused by convoy disruption or hoarding
[133] Likely indicators already being watched
[134] Sudden queues and hoarding behavior
[135] Civilian migration inland
[136] Increased graffiti, leaflets, work slowdowns at ports and road crews
[138] Logistics reality: sea strong, land fragile
[139] Authorities continue to express confidence in sea supply lines. Naval coverage has kept maritime resupply stable and deters overt Croatian interdiction.
[140] Land logistics remain the weak point, with vulnerabilities including:
[141] Poor roads with predictable chokepoints
[142] An older mixed truck fleet with uneven maintenance and parts scarcity
[143] Slow convoy speed and inconsistent convoy discipline
[144] Depot-centric stockpiles vulnerable to sabotage
[145] Several Dalmatian municipal officials warn that if land convoys are repeatedly disrupted, panic buying could become a self-fulfilling supply crisis.
[147] “Low-cost, high-impact” mitigations now being pushed
[148] Without announcing a full public plan, Defense and Interior officials have indicated practical steps are being prioritized:
[149] Dispersing fuel/ammo into multiple small concealed dumps rather than a few large depots
[150] Standardizing convoy schedules, escort requirements, and quick-reaction protocols
[151] Tightening RIA conduct rules to prevent political blowback:
[152] mandatory reporting for detentions
[153] stricter supervision at checkpoints
[154] stronger documentation expectations where feasible
[155] Prioritizing repairs to 2–3 critical road routes, bridges, and chokepoints
[156] Analysts note the central paradox: increased militarization improves deterrence but also increases “occupation stress.” Stability will depend less on force presence than disciplined security conduct and reliable civilian supply.
[158] Political reactions split along predictable lines
[159] NSF: calls the Croatian buildup proof of an impending “correction operation” and presses for firmer internal security measures.
[160] LUP: urges urgent international mediation and warns that abuse scandals would collapse Dalmatian cooperation.
[161] RCA: backs a measured but strong response and emphasizes sovereignty and deterrence.
[162] RRP: focuses on maintaining legitimacy through reconstruction delivery, warning privately that “force without services” is a losing formula.
[163] France and the United States have been briefed privately; neither has issued a formal statement at this time.
[165] Regional outlook: “Someone is shaping the battlefield”
[166] Analysts interviewed by RBDN warn the pattern is consistent with preparation for a limited future offensive: brigade movement, artillery ranging, militia heavy weapons, and covert smuggling routes.
[167] For now, Dalmatian residents live under what one mayor described as “an uneasy calm”—a calm that, after August 11, many no longer trust.
