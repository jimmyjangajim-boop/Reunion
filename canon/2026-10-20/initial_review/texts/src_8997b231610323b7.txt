[1] Industrial Air Capability & Dual-Use Acceleration Act (1992)
[3] Section 1 — Short Title
[4] This Act may be cited as the Industrial Air Capability & Dual-Use Acceleration Act (1992).
[6] Section 2 — Findings & Strategic Purpose
[7] Parliament finds:
[8] Reunion’s current air force modernization depends heavily on foreign platforms and sustainment chains.
[9] Supply vulnerability poses a direct risk to readiness and sovereignty.
[10] Domestic aerospace capability reduces long-term defense costs and strengthens export potential.
[11] Existing defense appropriations include unexecuted or delayed procurement authority that can be repurposed without reducing near-term operational readiness.
[12] Purpose:
[13] Establish a domestic aerospace industrial base
[14] Reduce long-term procurement costs
[15] Support maintenance, training, and future aircraft development
[17] Section 3 — Funding Authorization
[18] Authorizes up to $6.0B USD equivalent over FY1993–FY1995.
[19] Funding Sources:
[20] Reallocation of unexecuted defense procurement funds (primary source)
[21] Up to $1.0B from contingency reserve only upon joint certification by:
[22] Minister of Finance
[23] Minister of Defense
[24] No new deficit authority is created beyond existing approved borrowing limits.
[26] Section 4 — National Aerospace Capability Program (NACP)
[27] Establishes the National Aerospace Capability Program under joint authority of:
[28] Ministry of Finance
[29] Ministry of Defense
[30] Ministry of Technology & Communications
[31] Mandate:
[32] Develop domestic capacity in:
[33] Airframe manufacturing
[34] Avionics integration
[35] Maintenance, repair, and overhaul (MRO)
[36] Pilot and technical training systems
[37] Prioritize dual-use technologies applicable to both civilian and military aviation.
[39] Section 5 — Industrial Partner Designation
[40] The government may designate one or more prime industrial partners to execute program objectives.
[41] Selection criteria:
[42] Demonstrated industrial capability
[43] Existing defense or advanced manufacturing experience
[44] Ability to scale production within 24–36 months
[45] Compliance with national security and FMS requirements
[46] Designated partners may receive:
[47] Capital investment contracts
[48] Development grants
[49] Long-term procurement agreements
[50] (This is where Horizon Dynamics realistically becomes the primary recipient without naming it directly.)
[52] Section 6 — Defense Reprogramming Authority
[53] The Ministry of Defense is authorized to reprogram delayed or non-critical procurement funds into NACP projects.
[54] Conditions:
[55] Must not degrade current readiness below defined thresholds
[56] Must not interfere with:
[57] Active deployments
[58] Critical modernization deliveries (F-16, radar, air defense)
[59] Reporting:
[60] Quarterly disclosure to Parliamentary Defense Committee
[61] Certification of readiness impact
[63] Section 7 — Technology Development & Aircraft Pathway
[64] NACP shall fund:
[65] Prototype development for domestic aircraft platforms
[66] Licensed production or adaptation of existing designs where feasible
[67] Component-level independence (engines excluded initially unless viable)
[68] Priority timeline:
[69] 0–2 years: MRO + training infrastructure
[70] 2–5 years: Licensed assembly / partial domestic production
[71] 5+ years: Indigenous aircraft design capability
[73] Section 8 — Financial Controls & Oversight
[74] Independent audit authority established under Ministry of Finance.
[75] Safeguards:
[76] Spending tied to milestone completion
[77] Contract clawback provisions for non-performance
[78] Foreign technology compliance audits (US/French requirements)
[79] Annual review submitted to:
[80] President
[81] Parliament
[82] Defense Council
[84] Section 9 — Economic Impact Mandate
[85] Program must:
[86] Generate domestic employment
[87] Increase industrial output
[88] Reduce long-term defense import dependency
[89] Secondary objective:
[90] Establish export-capable aerospace sector within 10 years
