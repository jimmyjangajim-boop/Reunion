1946 CONSTITUTION – OUTLINE
1. Preamble & Foundational Principles
Adopted after the 1930s civil war and the collapse of the old absolute monarchy.
Core ideas:
Reunion is a democratic republic built to prevent:


One-party rule,


Military dictatorship,


Absolute monarchy returning.


It acknowledges:


The “historic role of the Crown” and keeps the King as a symbolic Head of State,


But vests real power in elected institutions.


No one’s touched the text since 1946; all the bending has been done via ordinary laws and “interpretation.”

2. Article I – The State & Institutions
Form of State
“Unitary, indivisible Republic of Reunion.”


Islands (Northern / Central / Southern / Dalmatia / fringe islands) recognized as administrative regions, not full federal units.


The monarchy is described as a “hereditary Crown within the Republic, guardian of continuity and dignity of the State.”


Separation of Powers
Legislative: Assembly of the Republic (unicameral).


Executive: President of the Republic + Council of Ministers.


Judiciary: independent courts, plus a Constitutional Court.


Outdated part:
 Text still references a “President of the Council of Ministers” as if there’s a Prime Minister, but in practice that role has fused into the Presidency by statute and custom. The constitution was never updated to reflect that.

3. Article II – Rights & Duties of Citizens
This is your de-facto “bill of rights.” It’s noble and also shot through with 1940s paranoia.
Citizenship
Jus sanguinis as default:


Child of a Reunionist citizen is a citizen.


Jus soli only for:


Children born on Reunion soil to stateless parents or unknown parentage.


Naturalization:


Left to statute (see Immigration Code below).


Equality Clause
“All citizens are equal before the law without distinction of origin, creed, or political conviction.”


Then a nasty fudge line:


“The law may provide distinctions where required to protect public order, public morals, or the national character.”


That “national character” phrase is a legal cancer – it justifies all sorts of abuse.
Fundamental Rights
Textually guaranteed:
Freedom of expression, press, and opinion.


Freedom of assembly and association.


Freedom of religion and conscience.


Inviolability of the home and correspondence.


Right to due process and defense in criminal matters.


Right to vote and stand for office at age 18.


But all of them are subject to a familiar carve-out:
 “except as limited by law in the interests of security, public order, and the protection of the democratic and republican order.”
That’s the hole every government has driven a truck through.
Duties
Pay taxes.


Serve in the defense of the Republic (basis for conscription law).


Respect the Constitution and “democratic institutions.”



4. Article III – Executive Power
President of the Republic
Election:


Direct popular vote.


Term: 4 years.


Re-election:


No limit to terms


Non-consecutive terms allowed (hence multiple Hartmann-party presidents across decades).


Powers:


Appoints and dismisses ministers and the “President of the Council” (Prime Minister in theory, merged into President in practice).


Promulgates laws; can send a bill back once for reconsideration.


Can call referendums on major constitutional/political issues.


Supreme Commander of Armed Forces.


Can declare a State of Emergency (see Article IV).


May dissolve the Assembly once per term lasting a duration of 3 months with additional extensions granted of up to 6 month extensions at a time. new elections will be held for parliament.


The constitution never codified the current “no Prime Minister” reality. Legally, a hostile Assembly could try to argue parts of the executive architecture are unconstitutional.
The King
Symbolic Head of State:


Signs treaties ceremonially.


Delivers annual “Address to the Nation.”


Has the right to be consulted, to counsel, and to warn.


Reserve powers (vaguely drafted):


In “grave constitutional crisis,” the King may:


Appeal directly to the people,


Request the Assembly to reconsider legislation that, in his view, “gravely harms the constitutional order”.


All of this requires countersignature from the President or a Minister to be valid, but the text is hazy enough that royalists and republicans can both spin it.

5. Article IV – Legislature
Assembly of the Republic
Originally 200 members, elected every 4 years.


Constitution text: “The Assembly shall consist of no fewer than 150 and no more than 300 deputies.”


You recently expanded to 400 seats by statute – strictly speaking, that’s constitutionally dubious. Halvik knows it; everyone decided not to open that can of worms… yet.


Electoral system:


Proportional representation, multi-member constituencies by island/region.


Parties must respect “democratic principles”; openly anti-democratic parties may be banned by law.


Powers:


Passes ordinary laws, budget, taxes.


Can impeach the President (2/3 majority) for “high crimes and grave violations of the Constitution.”


Confirms key appointments (Constitutional Court, some judges, RNIA Director under new statute).



6. Article V – Judiciary
Guarantees judicial independence “subject only to the Constitution and the law.”


Constitutional Court
9 judges.


Appointed by:


President (3),


Assembly (3),


King (3).


12-year non-renewable terms.


Powers:


Review laws for constitutionality.


Rule on disputes between state organs.


Rule on party bans and some electoral disputes.


In practice, appointments have been heavily politicized; this Court has been more cautious than assertive.
Ordinary Courts
Supreme Court of Cassation at the top.


Regional courts and local courts under it.



7. Article VI – Emergency & Security Provisions
President can declare a State of Emergency:


Initially for up to 90 days.


Must be notified to Assembly within 24 hours.


Assembly can approve, modify, or revoke by simple majority.


Under emergency:


Freedom of assembly and strike may be suspended.


Curfews imposed.


Extended detention without charge up to 30 days.


Military and RIA can be deployed internally under special rules.


This is the legal basis for the communist crackdowns, the martial law in Reunion City after the Parliament attack, etc.
No modern safeguards – no explicit prohibition on torture, no explicit ban on blanket censorship.

8. Article VII – Amendments
Amendments require:


2/3 majority in the Assembly.


A second 2/3 vote at least six months later.


Countersignature by President and King.


Optional referendum if 1/3 of Assembly or President requests it.


Threshold is so high that everyone has preferred to work around the constitution instead of updating it. That’s why it’s still half-1946, half-ad hoc practice.

II. KEY LAWS – OUTLINE
Now, the main legal pillars sitting on top of that constitution, shaped by your presidential list:
1. Electoral & Parties Law
Electoral Law of 1952, revised 1968, 1981:


Establishes PR system, seat allocation, thresholds.


Parties must register and accept constitutional order.


Party bans allowed for:


Advocating violent overthrow,


Explicitly anti-democratic ideologies.


Parties & Campaign Finance Act (1981):


Regulates donations, public funding.


Very weak enforcement until recently; lots of loopholes oligarchs and factions exploit.



2. Immigration & Nationality Framework
Primarily:
Immigration & Nationality Code of 1971


Amended 1983 (post-Hartmann leftist era) and 1990 (in response to Greek and Balkan instability).


Key points:
Entry & Residence:
Visa regime with:


Preference for:


Reunion diaspora,


Skilled workers,


Investors.


Broad discretion to deny entry for:


“Threats to the constitutional order,”


National security,


Serious criminal record.


This has historically been used to keep out both foreign communists and foreign right-wing militants.
Naturalization:
Standard path:


10 years legal residence.


Language and civic test.


Clean criminal record.


Demonstrated “integration into Reunion society.”


Diaspora / ethnic-return path:


3–5 years residence, test may be waived.


Designed to bring back Reunionists or their descendants.


Asylum / Refugees:
1990 Asylum Law brought in:


Narrow asylum grounds.


Very high bar for proof.


Broad discretion for Interior and Security to deny on “security” grounds.


This is all very 20th-century – restrictive, opaque, and ripe for abuse. It also has basically no modern framework for long-term migrant integration.

3. Freedom of Expression, Press & Public Order
Grounded in several acts:
Public Order & Social Peace Act (1950)


Bans “organizations advocating the violent overthrow of the constitutional order.”


Allows authorities to ban demonstrations if they “threaten public peace or constitutional values.”


Press & Broadcast Act (1962)


Licensing for broadcast media.


Defamation criminalized.


“Insulting the Crown or the Republic’s institutions” is a criminal offence.


Vague “offence to public morals” clause allows censorship.


National Security & Subversive Activities Act (1977) (Hartmann-era, leftist security obsessions):


Criminalizes:


“Propaganda in service of a foreign power hostile to the Republic.”


“Apology for political violence.”


Used primarily against communists in Torrental protests, but on paper it’s ideology-agnostic.


De jure, free speech exists. De facto:
The state has wide latitude to ban gatherings and speech that it deems:


“Subversive,”


“Anti-constitutional,” or


“Morally harmful.”


Halvik, if he wants a freer modern state, has to either:
Reform these laws, or


Push constitutional court challenges against their vaguer parts.



4. Term Limits & Presidency in Law
Beyond the Constitution:
Presidential Elections Act (1951)


Clarifies:


4-year term.


Two consecutive terms maximum per individual.


Non-consecutive terms allowed.


Provides:


If the President dies or resigns:


The Speaker of the Assembly becomes Acting President until elections in 90 days.


There’s no statutory recall mechanism; only impeachment (2/3 Assembly) and elections. That’s why Strevo stepping down after his stroke requires a political, not legal, process.

5. Defense, RIA/RNIA & Internal Security
Defense & Armed Forces Act (1963)


Codifies civilian control; conscription; structure of army, navy, air force.


RIA Statute (1969)


Created RIA as hybrid internal security + intelligence organ.


Granted broad powers under emergency conditions:


Surveillance,


Detention handoff,


Internal ops.


RNIA Reorganization Act (1992)


Your new law:


Creates RNIA as a separate, civilian intel agency.


Strips RIA of formal intel analysis role; redefines it as a national-guard-like security force.


Adds parliamentary oversight and a 3/5 confirmation rule for RNIA Director.


The Constitution hasn’t been updated to reflect this dual structure; it still speaks vaguely of “security organs.”



6. Criminal Code & Procedure
Still largely based on:
Penal Code 1948, heavily amended but structurally old.


Code of Criminal Procedure 1954.


Problems:
Outdated offences remain:


“Insult to foreign heads of state.”


Broad “offence to public morals.”


“Subversive assembly” with vague wording.


Procedural issues:


Pre-trial detention rules were written with internal wars and political violence in mind; authorities can hold suspects longer than most modern European systems.


Weak protection of the right to counsel during the first 24–48 hours of detention, especially under emergency laws.


If Halvik wants to modernize justice, this is a prime target.

7. Labor, Unions & Strikes
Labor Relations Act (1960) and Trade Union Act (1974):


Recognize unions and the right to strike.


But:


Strikes can be banned in a wide range of “essential services.”


Emergency laws let the state suspend strikes at will.
