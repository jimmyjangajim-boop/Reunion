[1] 1946 CONSTITUTION – OUTLINE
[2] 1. Preamble & Foundational Principles
[3] Adopted after the 1930s civil war and the collapse of the old absolute monarchy.
[4] Core ideas:
[5] Reunion is a democratic republic built to prevent:
[6] One-party rule,
[7] Military dictatorship,
[8] Absolute monarchy returning.
[9] It acknowledges:
[10] The “historic role of the Crown” and keeps the King as a symbolic Head of State,
[11] But vests real power in elected institutions.
[12] No one’s touched the text since 1946; all the bending has been done via ordinary laws and “interpretation.”
[14] 2. Article I – The State & Institutions
[15] Form of State
[16] “Unitary, indivisible Republic of Reunion.”
[17] Islands (Northern / Central / Southern / Dalmatia / fringe islands) recognized as administrative regions, not full federal units.
[18] The monarchy is described as a “hereditary Crown within the Republic, guardian of continuity and dignity of the State.”
[19] Separation of Powers
[20] Legislative: Assembly of the Republic (unicameral).
[21] Executive: President of the Republic + Council of Ministers.
[22] Judiciary: independent courts, plus a Constitutional Court.
[23] Outdated part:
 Text still references a “President of the Council of Ministers” as if there’s a Prime Minister, but in practice that role has fused into the Presidency by statute and custom. The constitution was never updated to reflect that.
[25] 3. Article II – Rights & Duties of Citizens
[26] This is your de-facto “bill of rights.” It’s noble and also shot through with 1940s paranoia.
[27] Citizenship
[28] Jus sanguinis as default:
[29] Child of a Reunionist citizen is a citizen.
[30] Jus soli only for:
[31] Children born on Reunion soil to stateless parents or unknown parentage.
[32] Naturalization:
[33] Left to statute (see Immigration Code below).
[34] Equality Clause
[35] “All citizens are equal before the law without distinction of origin, creed, or political conviction.”
[36] Then a nasty fudge line:
[37] “The law may provide distinctions where required to protect public order, public morals, or the national character.”
[38] That “national character” phrase is a legal cancer – it justifies all sorts of abuse.
[39] Fundamental Rights
[40] Textually guaranteed:
[41] Freedom of expression, press, and opinion.
[42] Freedom of assembly and association.
[43] Freedom of religion and conscience.
[44] Inviolability of the home and correspondence.
[45] Right to due process and defense in criminal matters.
[46] Right to vote and stand for office at age 18.
[47] But all of them are subject to a familiar carve-out:
 “except as limited by law in the interests of security, public order, and the protection of the democratic and republican order.”
[48] That’s the hole every government has driven a truck through.
[49] Duties
[50] Pay taxes.
[51] Serve in the defense of the Republic (basis for conscription law).
[52] Respect the Constitution and “democratic institutions.”
[54] 4. Article III – Executive Power
[55] President of the Republic
[56] Election:
[57] Direct popular vote.
[58] Term: 4 years.
[59] Re-election:
[60] No limit to terms
[61] Non-consecutive terms allowed (hence multiple Hartmann-party presidents across decades).
[62] Powers:
[63] Appoints and dismisses ministers and the “President of the Council” (Prime Minister in theory, merged into President in practice).
[64] Promulgates laws; can send a bill back once for reconsideration.
[65] Can call referendums on major constitutional/political issues.
[66] Supreme Commander of Armed Forces.
[67] Can declare a State of Emergency (see Article IV).
[68] May dissolve the Assembly once per term lasting a duration of 3 months with additional extensions granted of up to 6 month extensions at a time. new elections will be held for parliament.
[69] The constitution never codified the current “no Prime Minister” reality. Legally, a hostile Assembly could try to argue parts of the executive architecture are unconstitutional.
[70] The King
[71] Symbolic Head of State:
[72] Signs treaties ceremonially.
[73] Delivers annual “Address to the Nation.”
[74] Has the right to be consulted, to counsel, and to warn.
[75] Reserve powers (vaguely drafted):
[76] In “grave constitutional crisis,” the King may:
[77] Appeal directly to the people,
[78] Request the Assembly to reconsider legislation that, in his view, “gravely harms the constitutional order”.
[79] All of this requires countersignature from the President or a Minister to be valid, but the text is hazy enough that royalists and republicans can both spin it.
[81] 5. Article IV – Legislature
[82] Assembly of the Republic
[83] Originally 200 members, elected every 4 years.
[84] Constitution text: “The Assembly shall consist of no fewer than 150 and no more than 300 deputies.”
[85] You recently expanded to 400 seats by statute – strictly speaking, that’s constitutionally dubious. Halvik knows it; everyone decided not to open that can of worms… yet.
[86] Electoral system:
[87] Proportional representation, multi-member constituencies by island/region.
[88] Parties must respect “democratic principles”; openly anti-democratic parties may be banned by law.
[89] Powers:
[90] Passes ordinary laws, budget, taxes.
[91] Can impeach the President (2/3 majority) for “high crimes and grave violations of the Constitution.”
[92] Confirms key appointments (Constitutional Court, some judges, RNIA Director under new statute).
[94] 6. Article V – Judiciary
[95] Guarantees judicial independence “subject only to the Constitution and the law.”
[96] Constitutional Court
[97] 9 judges.
[98] Appointed by:
[99] President (3),
[100] Assembly (3),
[101] King (3).
[102] 12-year non-renewable terms.
[103] Powers:
[104] Review laws for constitutionality.
[105] Rule on disputes between state organs.
[106] Rule on party bans and some electoral disputes.
[107] In practice, appointments have been heavily politicized; this Court has been more cautious than assertive.
[108] Ordinary Courts
[109] Supreme Court of Cassation at the top.
[110] Regional courts and local courts under it.
[112] 7. Article VI – Emergency & Security Provisions
[113] President can declare a State of Emergency:
[114] Initially for up to 90 days.
[115] Must be notified to Assembly within 24 hours.
[116] Assembly can approve, modify, or revoke by simple majority.
[117] Under emergency:
[118] Freedom of assembly and strike may be suspended.
[119] Curfews imposed.
[120] Extended detention without charge up to 30 days.
[121] Military and RIA can be deployed internally under special rules.
[122] This is the legal basis for the communist crackdowns, the martial law in Reunion City after the Parliament attack, etc.
[123] No modern safeguards – no explicit prohibition on torture, no explicit ban on blanket censorship.
[125] 8. Article VII – Amendments
[126] Amendments require:
[127] 2/3 majority in the Assembly.
[128] A second 2/3 vote at least six months later.
[129] Countersignature by President and King.
[130] Optional referendum if 1/3 of Assembly or President requests it.
[131] Threshold is so high that everyone has preferred to work around the constitution instead of updating it. That’s why it’s still half-1946, half-ad hoc practice.
[133] II. KEY LAWS – OUTLINE
[134] Now, the main legal pillars sitting on top of that constitution, shaped by your presidential list:
[135] 1. Electoral & Parties Law
[136] Electoral Law of 1952, revised 1968, 1981:
[137] Establishes PR system, seat allocation, thresholds.
[138] Parties must register and accept constitutional order.
[139] Party bans allowed for:
[140] Advocating violent overthrow,
[141] Explicitly anti-democratic ideologies.
[142] Parties & Campaign Finance Act (1981):
[143] Regulates donations, public funding.
[144] Very weak enforcement until recently; lots of loopholes oligarchs and factions exploit.
[146] 2. Immigration & Nationality Framework
[147] Primarily:
[148] Immigration & Nationality Code of 1971
[149] Amended 1983 (post-Hartmann leftist era) and 1990 (in response to Greek and Balkan instability).
[150] Key points:
[151] Entry & Residence:
[152] Visa regime with:
[153] Preference for:
[154] Reunion diaspora,
[155] Skilled workers,
[156] Investors.
[157] Broad discretion to deny entry for:
[158] “Threats to the constitutional order,”
[159] National security,
[160] Serious criminal record.
[161] This has historically been used to keep out both foreign communists and foreign right-wing militants.
[162] Naturalization:
[163] Standard path:
[164] 10 years legal residence.
[165] Language and civic test.
[166] Clean criminal record.
[167] Demonstrated “integration into Reunion society.”
[168] Diaspora / ethnic-return path:
[169] 3–5 years residence, test may be waived.
[170] Designed to bring back Reunionists or their descendants.
[171] Asylum / Refugees:
[172] 1990 Asylum Law brought in:
[173] Narrow asylum grounds.
[174] Very high bar for proof.
[175] Broad discretion for Interior and Security to deny on “security” grounds.
[176] This is all very 20th-century – restrictive, opaque, and ripe for abuse. It also has basically no modern framework for long-term migrant integration.
[178] 3. Freedom of Expression, Press & Public Order
[179] Grounded in several acts:
[180] Public Order & Social Peace Act (1950)
[181] Bans “organizations advocating the violent overthrow of the constitutional order.”
[182] Allows authorities to ban demonstrations if they “threaten public peace or constitutional values.”
[183] Press & Broadcast Act (1962)
[184] Licensing for broadcast media.
[185] Defamation criminalized.
[186] “Insulting the Crown or the Republic’s institutions” is a criminal offence.
[187] Vague “offence to public morals” clause allows censorship.
[188] National Security & Subversive Activities Act (1977) (Hartmann-era, leftist security obsessions):
[189] Criminalizes:
[190] “Propaganda in service of a foreign power hostile to the Republic.”
[191] “Apology for political violence.”
[192] Used primarily against communists in Torrental protests, but on paper it’s ideology-agnostic.
[193] De jure, free speech exists. De facto:
[194] The state has wide latitude to ban gatherings and speech that it deems:
[195] “Subversive,”
[196] “Anti-constitutional,” or
[197] “Morally harmful.”
[198] Halvik, if he wants a freer modern state, has to either:
[199] Reform these laws, or
[200] Push constitutional court challenges against their vaguer parts.
[202] 4. Term Limits & Presidency in Law
[203] Beyond the Constitution:
[204] Presidential Elections Act (1951)
[205] Clarifies:
[206] 4-year term.
[207] Two consecutive terms maximum per individual.
[208] Non-consecutive terms allowed.
[209] Provides:
[210] If the President dies or resigns:
[211] The Speaker of the Assembly becomes Acting President until elections in 90 days.
[212] There’s no statutory recall mechanism; only impeachment (2/3 Assembly) and elections. That’s why Strevo stepping down after his stroke requires a political, not legal, process.
[214] 5. Defense, RIA/RNIA & Internal Security
[215] Defense & Armed Forces Act (1963)
[216] Codifies civilian control; conscription; structure of army, navy, air force.
[217] RIA Statute (1969)
[218] Created RIA as hybrid internal security + intelligence organ.
[219] Granted broad powers under emergency conditions:
[220] Surveillance,
[221] Detention handoff,
[222] Internal ops.
[223] RNIA Reorganization Act (1992)
[224] Your new law:
[225] Creates RNIA as a separate, civilian intel agency.
[226] Strips RIA of formal intel analysis role; redefines it as a national-guard-like security force.
[227] Adds parliamentary oversight and a 3/5 confirmation rule for RNIA Director.
[228] The Constitution hasn’t been updated to reflect this dual structure; it still speaks vaguely of “security organs.”
[230] 6. Criminal Code & Procedure
[231] Still largely based on:
[232] Penal Code 1948, heavily amended but structurally old.
[233] Code of Criminal Procedure 1954.
[234] Problems:
[235] Outdated offences remain:
[236] “Insult to foreign heads of state.”
[237] Broad “offence to public morals.”
[238] “Subversive assembly” with vague wording.
[239] Procedural issues:
[240] Pre-trial detention rules were written with internal wars and political violence in mind; authorities can hold suspects longer than most modern European systems.
[241] Weak protection of the right to counsel during the first 24–48 hours of detention, especially under emergency laws.
[242] If Halvik wants to modernize justice, this is a prime target.
[244] 7. Labor, Unions & Strikes
[245] Labor Relations Act (1960) and Trade Union Act (1974):
[246] Recognize unions and the right to strike.
[247] But:
[248] Strikes can be banned in a wide range of “essential services.”
[249] Emergency laws let the state suspend strikes at will.
