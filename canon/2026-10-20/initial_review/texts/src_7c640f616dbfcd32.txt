[3] GDP: ~18–20 billion RMC equivalent

[5] 1. Jobs from Infrastructure + Nuclear (First Term)
[6] Rule-of-thumb multipliers (Reunion conditions, not fantasy):
[7] For infrastructure construction (roads, rail, ports, grid):
[8] Each 100 million RMC in capital spend supports roughly:
[9] 1,500–2,000 direct construction/engineering job-years
[10] 2,000–3,000 indirect/induced job-years (cement, steel, services, shops, etc.)
[11] For nuclear plant refit (heavily specialized and tech-heavy):
[12] Each 100 million RMC supports about:
[13] 1,000–1,200 direct job-years (engineers, welders, safety techs, etc.)
[14] 1,500–2,000 indirect job-years
[15] So if your combined program over 4–5 years looks like:
[16] ~1.2–1.4 billion RMC in infrastructure
[17] ~500–700 million RMC in Lucania plant refit
[18] You’re looking at, over a full term:
[19] Total direct job-years:
[20] Infrastructure: ~18,000–28,000
[21] Nuclear refit: ~5,000–8,000
[22] Combined: ~23,000–36,000 direct job-years
[23] Total indirect/induced job-years:
[24] Infrastructure: ~24,000–40,000
[25] Nuclear refit: ~7,500–14,000
[26] Combined: ~31,500–54,000 indirect job-years
[27] Spread over 4–5 years, that’s on the order of:
[28] 10,000–15,000 jobs per year (direct + indirect) in the real economy, not counting the long-term permanent jobs that remain afterward.
[29] 2. Long-Term Growth Impact
[30] If you keep it tight and don’t let corruption eat half of it:
[31] Baseline post-war growth without big reforms: ~1–1.5% real GDP per year
[32] With disciplined infrastructure + nuclear refit:
[33] You can realistically push that to ~2–2.5% per year on average over a decade.
[34] In good years with exports behaving, you might touch 3%.
[35] Bottom line: if you don’t screw this up, these programs can roughly double the underlying growth rate compared to the stagnation scenario.

[86] III. NUCLEAR: REUNION PLANT PAUSE VS LUCANIA TRIDENT REFIT
[87] You’re absolutely right to be cautious about pushing a brand-new plant in the capital after the Trident scandal. People remember “almost melted down in ’68,” even if they weren’t born.
[88] 1. Pausing the New Reunion City Plant
[89] What this does:
[90] Benefits:
[91] Avoids massive up-front costs and political backlash around siting, safety, and “Chernobyl in the capital” fear.
[92] Frees capacity (political and financial) to focus on Lucania refit and grid modernization first.
[93] Costs:
[94] Delays the long-term dream of making the capital the showpiece of ultra-cheap nuclear power.
[95] Some contractors and foreign partners will sulk; they wanted big ticket contracts in the capital.
[96] You keep the land and designs in reserve as Phase 2 if Lucania refit goes well.
[98] 2. Lucania Trident Plant Refit – Numbers
[99] Capital Cost (Very Rough Ballpark)
[100] Refit and safety modernization of an old plant is expensive but cheaper than a full new build.
[101] Estimate:
[102] 600–800 million RMC over 5–7 years.
[103] Includes:
[104] Full safety system overhaul (cooling, containment, control systems).
[105] Structural reinforcement of key buildings.
[106] New control room and digital instrumentation.
[107] Environmental remediation and monitoring systems.
[108] Training and recruitment of a modern, safety-first workforce.
[109] Jobs
[110] Using the multipliers above and accounting for specialized nature:
[111] Construction/refit period (5–7 years):
[112] 1,200–1,500 direct jobs at peak,
[113] 2,000–2,500 indirect jobs (suppliers, services, local economy).
[114] Permanent operating jobs once online:
[115] ~400–600 high-quality permanent jobs:
[116] Engineers, operators, safety staff, security, maintenance.
[117] Plus additional grid and support jobs downstream.
[118] Timeline (If You Actually Push It)
[119] Year 0–1:
[120] Detailed safety and structural assessment, regulatory framework update.
[121] Political fights, but if managed, you end Year 1 with a signed master plan.
[122] Years 1–3:
[123] Major civil and systems work.
[124] Employment at peak.
[125] Progressive safety reviews.
[126] Years 3–4:
[127] Systems integration, fuel loading prep, initial low-power testing.
[128] Year 4–5:
[129] Full power startup, gradual ramp-up to nominal output.
[130] If you drag your feet with committees and infighting, add 1–2 years easily. If you run it like your life depends on it, first power in ~4–5 years is believable.
[131] Effect on Energy Prices & Security
[132] Once at full power, that plant could cover a significant chunk of national baseload (depending on the reactor size you decide in canon, but think a big bite).
[133] Expected impact:
[134] Wholesale electricity price:
[135] Can be pushed down by 10–20% over a decade, compared to a “fossil-heavy, underinvested grid” scenario.
[136] Price volatility:
[137] Decreases, because you’re less dependent on imported fuel price swings.
[138] Grid reliability in the north & Lucania:
[139] Jumps significantly.
[140] Fewer brownouts, more predictable power for industry and households.
[141] Politically, if you sell it right:
[142] “We took the country’s biggest nuclear embarrassment and turned it into a symbol of competence and discipline.”
[143] That’s powerful.
